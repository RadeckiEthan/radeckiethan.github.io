Abstract

This paper details machine learning techniques used for classification tasks related to movie review data. The dataset used was originally sourced from Rotten Tomatoes and annotated by Socher et al. (2013). A comprehensive sentiment analysis incorporating bag-of-words, sentiment lexicons (LIWC and Subjectivity), and negation handling features was designed and evaluated for classifying phrases into 5 different classes (negative to positive). Through progressive experimentation with varying preprocessing levels, feature combinations, and classifier models, Naive Bayes, Logistic Regression, and SVMs were compared across 1,000 to 10,000 samples from the dataset. The best model included bag-of-words, sentiment lexicons, and negation features in addition to a Logistic Regression classifier and minimal text preprocessing. This model achieved 58.1% accuracy and a 55.3% F-1 score on a sample size of 10,000, which was about a 7.7% improvement from the baseline model. These results validate findings from prior analysis by Pang et al. (2002), identifying SVM and Logistic Regression effectiveness over Naive Bayes. The negation features created during the process also showed positive impact over the baseline, demonstrating domain-specific information's importance to sentiment analysis.

Introduction

Fine-Grained Sentiment Classification

In an era where online reviews heavily influence consumer behavior, the ability to automatically detect sentiment in text has tremendous practical value for the entertainment industry and beyond. This project analyzes sentiment analysis on thousands of movie reviews from Rotten Tomatoes, with the goal of automatically predicting sentiment across five categories from positive to negative.

Most sentiment analysis systems use simple binary classification, labeling text as either positive or negative. However, real opinions are rarely that black and white. People express varying degrees of sentiment, from extremely negative to extremely positive, and everything in between. This project tackles the more challenging task of fine-grained sentiment classification, which involves distinguishing between five distinct levels of sentiment rather than just two. This approach better reflects how real-world critics would voice their opinions on movies, but also brings along tougher classification tasks.

As a result, accuracy rates are expected to be lower than those typically achieved in simple two-class classification tasks, with performance around 55-60% considered strong for this level of specificity.

Methodology

Data, Features, and Experimental Design

The dataset was originally sourced from Rotten Tomatoes movie reviews and annotated by Socher et al. (2013). The training set contains 156,060 phrases, each labeled with one of five sentiment categories. These labels were created through crowdsourcing, where annotators manually assigned sentiment scores to both complete sentences and sub-phrases within them. Random samples of 1,000, 5,000, and 10,000 phrases were drawn from the full training set due to computational constraints.

Two preprocessing strategies were compared throughout all experiments. The minimal preprocessing approach applied only tokenization, preserving everything else under the assumption that all textual elements might carry useful information. The full preprocessing approach applied lowercase conversion, stopword removal, and punctuation removal, with one critical exception: negation words such as "not", "never", and "no" were deliberately kept even though they appear on standard stopword lists. All experiments used 10-fold cross-validation, with F-1 score serving as the primary metric due to its balance between precision and recall in multi-class classification.

Feature 1
Bag of Words

The 2,000 most frequently occurring words across training data with binary presence features. Word presence was used rather than frequency, aligning with findings from Pang et al. (2002) showing presence outperforms frequency for sentiment tasks.

Feature 2
Sentiment Lexicons

Features drawn from LIWC (405 positive, 499 negative emotion words) and the Subjectivity Lexicon (2,718 positive, 4,912 negative, 570 neutral words). Raw counts and binary indicators were both included to capture sentiment-bearing vocabulary beyond what training data alone could provide.

Feature 3
Negation Handling

A custom feature detecting negation words then tracking all subsequent words until reaching punctuation. Sub-features included total negations, words in negation scope, and positive or negative sentiment words appearing after negation. For instance, in "not good" the feature registers one negation and one positive word being negated.

Feature 4
POS Tags

Part-of-speech tag counts for nouns, verbs, adjectives, and adverbs per phrase. Tested based on the idea that adjectives and adverbs carry more weight when describing sentiment. Ultimately found to add noise rather than signal for this task.

Three classifiers were compared: NLTK's Naive Bayes as the probabilistic baseline, Scikit-learn's Logistic Regression configured with a maximum of 1,000 training iterations, and Scikit-learn's Linear SVM. The experimental design followed a progressive strategy, starting simple and gradually adding complexity, with all three classifiers tested on the best-performing feature combination at the end.

Experiments and Results

Progressive Feature Experimentation

All experiments utilized 10-fold cross-validation and performance was evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, and F-1 score, with F-1 score serving as the primary metric due to its balance between precision and recall in multi-class classification problems.

Experiment 1 · Bag of Words Baseline

Since bag-of-words was the only feature within this model, the evaluation metrics were relatively low, but that is expected with a multi-class classification task. Full preprocessing works better in this experiment, as this is the most simple of all the experiments within this paper.

Figure 1. Bag of Words baseline results. Full preprocessing outperforms minimal at this stage, with F-1 scores of 45.60% vs 42.50%.

Experiment 2 · Sentiment Lexicons Only

This model removed bag-of-words features and evaluated sentiment lexicon features alone. Accuracy and recall decreased from the first model, but precision and F-1 took a big step up, which makes sense since the sentiment lexicons are better suited for identifying nuances within the phrases and correctly classifying all classes rather than just the extreme or neutral phrases.

Figure 2. Sentiment Lexicons only results. Precision and F-1 improve over BoW despite lower accuracy, showing the lexicons capture nuance that raw word counts miss.

Experiment 3 · BoW + Sentiment Lexicons

Combining the bag-of-words and sentiment lexicon features produced decent improvement from the individual models, emphasizing that these two feature types stack well within sentiment analysis. The F-1 score jumped to 54.2% for full preprocessing, with the previous high being 49.5%. It is also interesting to note that minimal and full preprocessing models are performing similarly at this point.

Figure 3. BoW + Sentiment Lexicons combined. F-1 jumps to 54.2%, confirming these two feature types stack well for sentiment analysis.

Experiment 4 · BoW + Sentiment Lexicons + Negation

Adding custom negation features to the baseline performed better, showing that negation is an important feature for sentiment analysis. Minimal preprocessing performed better than full preprocessing across the board for the first time, hinting that more complex models benefit from less preprocessing.

Figure 4. BoW + Sentiment Lexicons + Negation. Best feature model so far, with minimal preprocessing outperforming full for the first time (+0.9% accuracy, +1.0% F-1 over BoW + SL).

Experiments 5 and 6 · POS Tags and All Features

Substituting negation for POS tags performed worse than the prior model, suggesting POS tags are not as helpful as expected when completing sentiment analysis. The F-1 score decreased by around 1% while other metrics decreased between 0.6% and 1.6%. Combining all features including both POS tags and negation did not reach the same performance level that negation features alone achieved, showing that stacking in this scenario does not help and that POS may not be useful for these tasks.

Figure 5. BoW + SL + POS Tags. Performance drops compared to negation model, suggesting grammatical structure is less informative than lexical content for sentiment.

Figure 6. All features combined. Stacking POS with negation does not improve over negation alone, confirming POS tags add noise rather than signal.

Experiment 7 · Classifier Comparison

All three classifiers were evaluated on the best feature combination (BoW, sentiment lexicons, and negation features) across minimal and full preprocessing on 10,000 samples. Minimal preprocessing clearly works better for these complex classifiers and Logistic Regression and SVM are better for multi-class classification than Naive Bayes. The best configuration identified was Logistic Regression with minimal preprocessing, achieving 58.1% accuracy and 55.3% F-1.

Figure 7. Classifier comparison on the best feature set. Logistic Regression and SVM outperform Naive Bayes by +2.3% accuracy and +2.5% F-1 under minimal preprocessing.

Summary of Results

The full progression from baseline to final model under minimal preprocessing shows consistent improvement with each meaningful feature addition, with POS tags being the one feature that did not contribute positively.

Figure 8. Full results summary. Total improvement of 7.7% accuracy and 12.8% F-1 from the BoW baseline to the final Logistic Regression model under minimal preprocessing.

Figure 9. Performance progression from baseline to final model (minimal preprocessing). Each meaningful feature addition produced incremental gains in both accuracy and F-1 score.

Figure 10. Confusion matrix for the final Logistic Regression model. The neutral class dominates predictions, reflecting the dataset's class imbalance where neutral phrases account for approximately 51% of samples.

Discussion

Key Findings and Unexpected Results

The results demonstrate that both feature engineering and classifier selection significantly impact sentiment classification performance. The best-performing model included minimal preprocessing with Logistic Regression using bag-of-words, sentiment lexicons, and negation features. The model achieved 58.1% accuracy on the five-class classification task, representing a 7.7% improvement over the baseline. The model also achieved 55.3% F-1, representing a 12.8% improvement over the baseline. While this overall accuracy and F-1 may appear modest compared to binary classification benchmarks, it represents strong performance for fine-grained sentiment analysis where distinguishing between adjacent sentiment levels is very difficult.

The most surprising finding was that POS tag features decreased rather than improved performance, suggesting grammatical structure is less informative than lexical content for sentiment. Minimal preprocessing outperformed full preprocessing for advanced models, which contradicts the common practice of aggressive text cleaning, inferring that stopwords and punctuation may be important for sentiment analysis. The 2.3% performance gain of Logistic Regression over Naive Bayes aligns with findings from Pang et al. (2002), validating that more sophisticated classifiers better capture sentiment nuances.

Challenges

The dataset's class distribution created challenges throughout, with neutral phrases accounting for approximately 51% of the full 156k sample, negative classes around 22%, and positive classes around 27%. This imbalance caused the model to over-predict the neutral class, as visible in the confusion matrix. Key limitations also include computational constraints that restricted experiments to 10,000 samples rather than the full training dataset, and the absence of bigram or trigram features that could better capture multi-word expressions like "not good."

Figure 11. Unigram vs bigram tokenization. The current unigram approach splits "not good" into separate tokens, treating "good" as positive. A bigram approach would capture "not good" as a single negative unit, which would improve sentiment reversal handling.

Conclusion

Strong Performance for a Challenging Five-Class Task

This project demonstrated effective approaches for multi-class sentiment classification on movie review phrases. The final model achieved 58.1% accuracy with 55.3% F-1 score, representing strong performance for five-class sentiment analysis without signs of overtraining. Cross-validation results remained consistent across 10-folds, and performance scaled appropriately with dataset size.

Alternative approaches worth exploring include deep learning methods like BERT for contextual understanding and bigram or trigram features to capture multi-word expressions. Future analysis could also look into altering the data to be a binary classification rather than five-class classification to analyze model performance with less nuance in the data.

Core Finding

The best model combined bag-of-words, sentiment lexicons, and custom negation features with a Logistic Regression classifier under minimal preprocessing, achieving 58.1% accuracy and 55.3% F-1 score on 10,000 samples across 10-fold cross-validation. This represents a 7.7% accuracy improvement and 12.8% F-1 improvement over the bag-of-words baseline, validating that domain-specific feature engineering and classifier selection both meaningfully impact fine-grained sentiment classification performance.
