Abstract

This paper explores the multifaceted relationship between NBA player statistics, contract valuation, and the league's evolving salary cap structure. Drawing from a wide range of analytical models and scholarly articles, the research examines which performance metrics, ranging from basic counting stats like points per game to advanced metrics like PER and true shooting percentage, most significantly influence contract size. Additionally, this paper evaluates how the 2023 NBA Collective Bargaining Agreement (CBA) has reshaped cap management strategies, with new rules regarding punitive thresholds like the First and Second Aprons. By synthesizing findings from both academic and industry sources, the paper highlights how teams now balance statistical evaluation with financial constraints to optimize roster construction and player compensation. Ultimately, the research underscores the NBA's shift toward more data-informed decision-making while promoting equitable pay structures under the revised CBA.

Introduction

How the 2023 CBA Changed Everything

Before the 2011-12 NBA season, the league entered a lockout while negotiating a new Collective Bargaining Agreement that would provide fair terms for both players and teams. Since then, each successive CBA has added new layers of complexity to team-building decisions, culminating in the 2023 CBA, a major topic of discussion due to its stricter financial penalties and expanded salary cap restrictions.

This paper seeks to understand which types of players are rewarded with larger shares of team salary and which team-level strategies most effectively lead to regular-season success. To answer these questions, three models were developed. The first examines how individual characteristics such as position, age, and performance metrics affect a player's share of team salary. The second analyzes which team playstyle indicators are most strongly associated with win percentage. The third compares the relative impact of offensive versus defensive performance on team success.

Data was sourced from Basketball-Reference.com and Basketball.RealGM.com, covering the 2011-12 season through 2024-25. The sample was limited to players who participated in at least the average number of games and minutes per game during the sample period: 41 games and 18.9 minutes per game. These filters yielded 3,176 NBA player-seasons across 420 team rosters, focusing on players typically included in a team's active rotation whose salaries reflect intentional roster-building decisions.

What the Data Shows Before Modeling

Visualizing the data helps produce an understanding of how independent variables may affect dependent variables in the models, like contract size or team success. The average Offensive Rating in the NBA during the 2011-12 season was 104.53 and increased to 114.95 by the 2023-24 season, reflecting the league-wide shift toward pace and three-point volume that makes season-adjusted metrics essential throughout this analysis.

Scoring and Salary Allocation

Points per Game has a strong positive correlation with the percentage of team salary taken up by a given player. This suggests that players who score more will most likely earn a greater salary. Notable examples include prime James Harden earning 25-30% of the Houston Rockets' total salary, and Luka Doncic and Trae Young during their second NBA seasons significantly outperforming their expected scoring relative to their salaries, highlighting the importance of teams maximizing value from rookie-scale contract players.

Advanced Metrics and Salary Allocation

Both Offensive Box Plus-Minus (OBPM) and Defensive Box Plus-Minus (DBPM) exhibit positive relationships with salary, yet OBPM demonstrates a stronger correlation. OBPM primarily reflects individual offensive performance, whereas DBPM is more indicative of a player's impact on team defense. The figure highlights examples of players who either underperform or exceed expectations relative to their salary, such as Lance Stephenson falling below expectations and Kawhi Leonard exceeding them. Similarly, Draymond Green's elite defensive capabilities combined with his relatively modest salary facilitated the Golden State Warriors' sustained success, including multiple NBA Finals appearances.

How Positional Value Has Shifted

The market for NBA players by position has evolved, reflecting changes in offensive and defensive strategies, with the rise of three-point shooting and faster pace of play being particularly influential. Between 2011 and 2015, the average share of a team's total salary allocated to a rotational point guard was approximately 7.44%, but from 2020 to 2025 this share had risen to nearly 10.69%. In contrast, the salary share for a rotational center, which stood at 11.75% from 2011 to 2015, decreased to about 9.74% in the more recent period.

Figure 7. Average Salary Share by Position across three time periods (2011-2015, 2015-2020, 2020-2025). Point guard value has risen sharply while center value has declined, reflecting the league's shift toward pace and space.

Team Age, Roster Construction, and Sustained Success

The average age on a roster for rotational players between 2011 and 2025 is about 26.4 years old, aligning with the fact that NBA players typically reach peak performance in their late 20s. The following panels highlight select franchises that illustrate contrasting roster construction philosophies and their outcomes.

Both the Los Angeles Clippers and Oklahoma City Thunder sustained success over the past 14 seasons through very different approaches. The Clippers' rotation has been older than the league average every season since 2011, reflecting a preference for acquiring established veterans. Remarkably, the Clippers are the only team in the 14-year sample to post a win percentage above 50% every season. The Thunder, on the other hand, leaned heavily on the draft, securing franchise talents like Kevin Durant, Russell Westbrook, and more recently Chet Holmgren and Jalen Williams on rookie-scale contracts, allowing them to spend elsewhere on proven veterans.

The Sacramento Kings and San Antonio Spurs illustrate both the risks and rewards of rebuilding. The Kings have posted a winning record in only two seasons during this span despite rarely fielding a particularly young core. The Spurs, after averaging a 71.15% win percentage with Tim Duncan and Kawhi Leonard from 2011-12 through 2017-18, quickly declined after Leonard's departure and have since committed to a youth-driven rebuild centered on Victor Wembanyama and Stephon Castle on rookie-scale contracts, a path followed successfully by franchises like the 76ers, Suns, and Cavaliers in the preceding decade.

Model 1 · Player Contract Evaluation

The final model explains approximately 56.1% of the variation in a player's share of team salary and is statistically significant overall. Composite advanced statistics such as PER, WS/48, OBPM, and DBPM were removed after several iterations, driven by the recognition that front offices are more likely to base salary decisions on a player's visible, in-game impact rather than complex advanced metrics. Key findings reveal that age and offensive production metrics, notably Points and Assists per Game, have the strongest influence on a player's share of team salary. For each additional point or assist per game, a player is expected to earn 0.571% and 0.535% more of their team's salary cap respectively, holding all else constant. Most notably, a point guard in the 2020-2025 era is expected to earn 3.159% more of a team's salary cap than a center in the 2011-2015 era, highlighting how positional value and team-building strategies have evolved.

Model 2 · Team Playstyle and Win Percentage

The model is statistically significant and explains 91.3% of the variance in team win percentage, indicating a strong fit. Notably, a one-unit increase in Turnover Percentage is associated with a 4.3 percentage point decrease in win percentage, holding all else constant. In contrast, a one-unit increase in Opponent Turnover Percentage yields only a 3.1 percentage point increase in win percentage. This asymmetry suggests that the negative impact of committing turnovers outweighs the benefits of forcing them, highlighting the strategic value of ball security over defensive aggression. Regarding team spending, each 1% increase in spending relative to the league cap is associated with a 0.042% increase in win percentage. It is noteworthy that 3-Point Attempt Rate and 3-Point Percentage are not statistically significant in the model, indicating that no definitive conclusions can be drawn regarding the impact of three-point shooting on team success on a year-to-year basis.

Table 7. OLS Regression Results for Team Playstyle and Team Success. Dependent variable: Team Win Percentage. N = 420, R² = 0.913, F = 431.444*** (df = 10; 409).

Model 3 · Offense vs Defense and Win Percentage

The model includes only three variables but explains 93.3% of the variance in win percentage. All three variables as well as the overall model are statistically significant at the 1% level. Regarding the key question of whether offense or defense is more influential in determining win percentage relative to league averages, the results indicate that net defensive rating has a slightly greater effect. A one-unit increase in net defensive rating (relative to league average) is associated with an expected 2.896% increase in win percentage, compared to a 2.872% increase for net offensive rating, holding all else constant. Thus, defensive performance relative to the league appears to be slightly more impactful than offensive performance when it comes to predicting team success.

Conclusion

Offense Earns Contracts, Defense Wins Games

This paper aimed to examine which roster construction strategies most effectively position NBA teams for sustained success within the constraints of the modern salary cap and collective bargaining agreements.

Age and offensive production drive individual contracts. Points and Assists per Game are the most influential factors in determining a player's share of team salary, and results suggest a growing valuation of point guards in the modern NBA, highlighting a broader league-wide shift in team-building priorities that favor offensive-minded guards.

Ball security matters more than disruption. Limiting turnovers emerged as more critical to success than forcing them, and aggressive offensive strategies such as drawing fouls and dominating the glass were positively associated with win percentage.

Three-point shooting is not a significant predictor of year-to-year success. No statistically significant relationship was found between three-point shooting volume or accuracy and team success on an annual basis, despite the league's widespread adoption of the strategy.

Defense wins, slightly. While both are important, having a stronger defense relative to the league average contributes slightly more to winning than having a stronger offense, with net defensive rating producing a 2.896% improvement in win percentage per unit versus 2.872% for net offensive rating.

Core Finding

While offensive skill and guard play are highly valued in individual contracts, team success is more strongly tied to disciplined, defense-oriented strategies. The data suggests that while individual contracts heavily favor offensive production and guard play, sustainable team success is more closely tied to defensive strength, rebounding, and disciplined execution.
