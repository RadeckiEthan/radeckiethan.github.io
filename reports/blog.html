Two Years in Applied Data Science
A Program Reflection

Ethan Radecki M.S. Applied Data Science Syracuse University · 2026

Program Expectations

As an undergraduate studying Sport Analytics and Economics at Syracuse University, I believed that I was going to get a job in sports data as soon as I graduated college. That all changed during my freshman year spring semester when I found out about the Orange Forever Scholarship. This scholarship enabled me to focus on graduating undergraduate studies in 3 years with the plan to study at the iSchool for an extra year to earn my master's in Applied Data Science. As a person who loves data science, wanted to maximize my time at Syracuse, and save as much money as possible, the 3+1 program was an easy decision. I finished my undergraduate studies in May of 2025 and this past fall and spring I have been a part of the A.D.S. program at the iSchool.

In terms of expectations for the program and what material I would learn, I came in thinking I would expand my Python abilities while mastering other languages and software like R and SQL. Beyond those technical expectations, I thought this program would make me a better communicator and presenter. While applying for jobs and completing interviews the past couple of years, I often get asked about my ability to communicate technical methodology and findings to non-technical audiences. In the past I would answer that question with some confidence based on prior experiences and classes, but I was never fully certain I would be supremely effective at it. I also expected to learn more about the possibilities of my skills and education within the workforce. After graduating with degrees in Sport Analytics and Economics, I didn't think I'd be able to get a job outside of sports even if I wanted to, so joining the A.D.S. program was partly about becoming less sports-specific while still having that sports background. To put it simply, I expected to learn what was possible with this master's degree, not just in sport, but in any field I have an interest in.

One final area I expected to learn about was Artificial Intelligence, which is why I chose to complete my concentration in A.I. I used LLMs and GenAI like ChatGPT throughout my undergraduate studies, but I wanted to learn how these models are built, how they are able to run and provide feedback in a timely manner, and how I could potentially create machine learning or deep learning models to complete complex data analysis that could push the frontier of data science in the coming years.

I want to talk specifically about my expectations for programming languages because those skills have become the base of who I am professionally, while also bleeding into my personal life as seen in a couple of the projects on this portfolio. When I first entered the program my expertise was in R, with a little Python experience from two Sport Analytics classes and one SQL class through the iSchool. I expected a lot more Python and SQL, which is what I got, but I did not think my Python confidence would exceed my R confidence by the end of my time at Syracuse. I gained solid SQL experience but assumed the program would be more SQL-based than Python-based, and that is where my expectations did not align with reality. This could be because I chose more Python-oriented classes, but I also think the iSchool is evolving to match the ever-changing landscape of data science with complex deep learning models that can be built in Python but not in a query-based language like SQL.

Overall, my year in the program met the expectations I had coming in, and if anything my experience sidestepped rather than failed certain expectations, like the Python and SQL balance. I am also happy with how the program taught me about the data science industry and how it continues to evolve with Artificial Intelligence at the forefront.

How I Achieved Each Learning Outcome

1. Collect, store, and access data by identifying and leveraging applicable technologies

Data collection was a meaningful and deliberate part of every project in this portfolio. For the housing market project, the panel data consisting of 24 years across twenty U.S. metro regions was pulled from the FRED database. For the Letterboxd project, I exported rating and review data from my account and then merged it with genre, director, actor, and financial metadata pulled from the TMDB API using a pipeline I built. In each case, figuring out how to get the right data cleanly into a usable shape was a real and significant part of the process.

2. Create actionable insight across a range of contexts using the full data science life cycle

The projects in this portfolio span sports, economics, entertainment, and personal analytics, which reflects what I find interesting about data science. Each project follows the full lifecycle from collecting data to drawing and reporting conclusions, whether that is identifying a decoupling effect in housing markets, identifying what drives NBA player evaluation, or attempting to build an accurate emotion recognition system to streamline music decisions for users.

3. Apply visualization and predictive models to help generate actionable insight

Visualization and modeling show up in every project but in different ways depending on what the question called for. The Letterboxd project leans on interactive charts and dashboards, while the fantasy football project uses Python-generated charts and visuals. The NBA roster construction project pairs OLS regression results with Tableau dashboards, and the housing market project uses both Bayesian and frequentist approaches in R. In each case the model or chart exists to answer a specific question rather than to demonstrate a technique.

4. Use programming languages such as R and Python to support the generation of actionable insight

R was my primary tool for the housing market and NBA roster construction projects, while Python was the main language for the Letterboxd project, movie review sentiment project, fantasy football platform, and CNN emotion project. Using both languages across many different projects has significantly boosted my confidence with each one, alongside the coursework in the Applied Data Science program.

5. Communicate insights gained via visualization and analytics to a broad range of audiences

Communication was something I thought about intentionally across the portfolio. Each project has a report page designed to be readable by someone without a data science background, and the fantasy football platform was built specifically for league members who just wanted to understand their season better. Each project page shares a consistent structure with conclusions and findings presented clearly, while the visualizations and analysis throughout serve as the backbone for those findings.

6. Apply ethics in the development, use, and evaluation of data and predictive models

The CNN emotion project is where ethics came up most directly, with dataset imbalance and grayscale image formats creating reliability issues for certain emotion classes and users with darker skin tones. Those limitations are acknowledged openly in the report because context matters for understanding what the model can and cannot do. The housing market project reports findings as associations rather than causal claims, and the Letterboxd project avoids overclaiming what the NLP models are demonstrating. Across all projects in this portfolio, I aimed to be honest and respectful about what the data shows and how the conclusions should be interpreted.

Three Projects and How They Contributed to My Education

The three projects I have pulled from my coursework during my time in the Applied Data Science program are Decoupling of U.S. Housing Markets from Economic Fundamentals, Emotion to Music Recommender Using CNNs and OpenCV, and Movie Review Sentiment Analysis, all of which can be found within this portfolio.

The housing markets project comes from IST 686 – Quantitative Reasoning for Data Science, and the focus was to identify how house prices have changed since 2000 and what the greatest predictors of that change are. After analyzing the data and completing visual and regression analysis, I determined that the main takeaway was the decoupling effect between house pricing and unemployment rates from 2000 to 2024. I used both frequentist and Bayesian approaches to confirm a 97% decline in unemployment sensitivity by the Covid period across three defined time periods: Pre-Crisis, Recovery, and Covid. The analysis was completed entirely in R, which allowed me to refine my R knowledge into something I am proud of. This project was pivotal to my education because it forced me to explore many analysis routes within R while also focusing on the full data project pipeline from conception to data collection to analysis to communicating findings to non-technical stakeholders.

The emotion to music recommendation CNN project comes from IST 691 – Deep Learning in Practice, and was focused on building a convolutional neural network and pipeline to analyze user sentiment based on a screenshot of their face and then recommend a song that matches that sentiment. The idea is novel and very doable in the modern world of deep learning. As discussed in the project report, the results were not as accurate as we would have liked, but with more time and resources I think it could be a genuinely interesting addition to the music industry. This project contributed to my education because it allowed me to put my deep learning knowledge to the test and design, build, train, and push a convolutional neural network on my own. IST 691 had a lot of rich content, but the final project was crucial for letting me get my hands dirty with the material and work through real challenges in practice.

The movie review sentiment project comes from IST 664 – Natural Language Processing and had the goal of accurately classifying Rotten Tomatoes movie reviews into one of five sentiment groups: Negative, Somewhat Negative, Neutral, Somewhat Positive, and Positive. As one might expect, the models had a difficult time achieving the usual high accuracy benchmarks since five classes and imbalanced data made training and testing more challenging. The analysis identified optimal features for sentiment classification and compared three classifiers including SVM, Naive Bayes, and Logistic Regression, with Logistic Regression coming out on top. This project was crucial for my education in natural language processing because it allowed me to apply class material to real-world data in an industry I find interesting, which is why I continued some of that sentiment analysis in another project in this portfolio.

All three projects contributed to my education because they allowed me to use real-world data, explore industries I care about, and complete analysis that I did not think was possible before taking each of their respective courses.

Projects Outside the Classroom

I did not participate in a professional internship or external projects while in the Applied Data Science program, but I did complete a few personal projects enabled by my studies in the program. The two projects I completed on my own time that were influenced by material and tools I learned here are My Letterboxd in Numbers and the Fantasy Sports Analytics Platform, both of which are semi-ongoing. The Letterboxd project is updated every month and the fantasy football platform is updated weekly during the NFL season.

The Letterboxd project is a personal data analytics project that serves as a sort of playground for me to analyze my own data and apply some of the natural language processing techniques I learned in IST 664. Before that class I had no idea how someone could take a piece of writing and analyze it in a way that determines sentiment or summarizes a massive document into a small paragraph. The class opened my eyes to what was possible. I say this project serves as a continuation of my education rather than a direct influence on it because it keeps me researching NLP and forces me to stay current in the field while completing analysis on data that is personally meaningful to me.

The fantasy football project introduced me to scripting techniques in Python while I was simultaneously taking IST 652 – Scripting for Data Analysis. The simultaneous experience of using Python to pull data, clean and format it, run analysis, generate charts, and push everything to a live website while taking that course was incredibly useful and led to me having an easy time in class. The platform was also built to be used by real people, which pushed me to think carefully about how the findings would be communicated to league members who are not data scientists.

Overall, I think using the material and tools from this program on personal projects in fields I am passionate about is a great way to not only learn but continue to grow as a data scientist.

Favorite Classes and Why

During my time in the Applied Data Science program I had two classes that stood above the rest. I cannot decide which one I liked more so I will talk about both and explain how each made me a better data scientist in separate ways. The two classes are IST 691 – Deep Learning in Practice and FIN 654 – Financial Analytics.

Deep Learning in Practice was one of the first classes I took in the A.D.S. program and the most eye-opening course I have taken in all my time at Syracuse University. The material was entirely new to me when I walked in, but I knew it would be an interest of mine because I had always been curious about how LLMs and GenAI actually worked under the hood. Learning how powerful Python could be for modeling and how deep learning architectures are built was so important to me and has shown throughout my work ever since. Beyond the material, Professor Dunham is one of the best teachers I have had in four years at Syracuse. He is respectful, patient, always looking for ways to improve his teaching, and knew how to move through topics so that everyone had their questions answered by the end of each class. IST 691 was my favorite iSchool course for all of those reasons and I would recommend it to anyone with even a passing interest in data or artificial intelligence.

My other favorite class was Financial Analytics, which I know is not through the iSchool, but I feel I have to mention it because it allowed me to branch out and apply my data science tools to a completely new domain. The class was a great refresher on R and modeling, using OLS, logistic regression, and decision trees on real finance data. As I mentioned earlier, one of my goals coming into this program was to expand my career capabilities beyond sport, with finance being one of the fields I was most curious about. I had not taken a single finance class as an undergraduate so a lot of the material was new to me, but that is what made the course so great. Professor Jens cares deeply about her students and makes it a goal that every student understands the work both inside and outside the classroom. She also had a thoughtful and balanced mindset toward Artificial Intelligence, viewing it as a guide and learning tool rather than something to avoid entirely, which I think reflects the kind of adaptable thinking that makes someone an excellent teacher and practitioner.

Both IST 691 and FIN 654 stood out for their combination of interesting material and excellent teaching, and I would recommend either to any student with an interest in data, finance, or artificial intelligence.

The Best Parts and the Biggest Surprises

The best part of the A.D.S. program are the teachers, specifically the knowledge and genuine care they bring to their students. I had the pleasure of learning from ten different professors during my time in the program and each one had their own approach, but each one excelled in their own way. I talked about two of my favorites above, but I could speak the same way about at least three others. Whether I was struggling with the material or it was easy sailing, the teachers were always there to support me and make sure I understood the content, and that consistency made a real difference in how I experienced the program.

In terms of biggest surprises, two come to mind. The first is the range of different class fields the iSchool offers. When I entered the program I did not think about the possibility of completing an Artificial Intelligence concentration or joining a class focused on cloud management. I came in expecting to sharpen my data analysis skills and generalize myself beyond sport, but my education went much further than that and made me a more complete data scientist across fields I had never considered.

The second surprise was the diversity of the student body. I did not know what to expect going in, but I was genuinely impressed by the range of cultures and backgrounds represented across my classes. Syracuse has always been good to me in terms of meeting new people and being exposed to new perspectives, but my time in the A.D.S. program expanded on that with international students and classmates from across the country, many of whom I had the opportunity to work with during final projects. Those connections were unexpected and made the experience richer in ways I did not anticipate.

Overall, the teachers were the focal point of my learning experience, I discovered fields I never planned to study, and the connections I made with people from diverse backgrounds were one of the biggest and best surprises of the entire program.
