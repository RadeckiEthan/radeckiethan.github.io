Abstract

Traditionally, housing markets across the U.S. have responded to economic fundamentals, like unemployment and housing supply. This paper explores these relationships and how they have shifted from 2000 to 2024. The analysis was completed using monthly national metrics in addition to house price indices from 20 different metro regions across the U.S. Both a frequentist and Bayesian approach were applied to the analysis, which included period interactions to identify fluctuations in market sensitivity across two crises. The results revealed three distinct periods based on economic fundamentals' relationships with housing prices, specifically unemployment's relationship. During the Pre-Crisis period (2000-2008) there appeared to be a strong sensitivity to unemployment rates (-2.19% effect per percentage point increase in unemployment), the Recovery period (2009-2019) showed a deterioration of this effect (-1.23%), and by the Covid period (2020-2024) this relationship was nearly decoupled (-0.06%). Across the three periods, the unemployment rate's predictive power over housing prices across the nation on a monthly basis had declined by around 97%. This finding was supported by both the frequentist and Bayesian approaches. These results suggest that fiscal and monetary policy intervention measures, not accounted for in this analysis, very well may have altered housing market dynamics since 2000, suggesting a decoupling effect between unemployment and housing prices.

Introduction

Background and Motivation

It is generally accepted that housing markets respond to economic fundamentals, like unemployment and housing supply. For example, if unemployment rises we would expect demand for housing to decrease as fewer consumers have disposable income. Similarly, if housing supply were to increase through construction we would expect prices to decrease as competition rises, although demand for housing would need to not exceed supply for this effect to take place. Still, these relationships are the foundation of housing market forecasts and policy interventions.

Since 2000 we have witnessed two major crises that have brought huge impacts to the housing market: the 2008 housing bubble and the 2020 Covid pandemic. These two crises are the root of this paper's central analysis, attempting to identify how these relationships have been altered across these periods, and debating whether the relationships even persist through 2024.

Understanding how these relationships have changed throughout time and during crises is crucial for policymakers and consumers that want to manage their potential risk in the housing market. If there truly is a decoupling effect between economic fundamentals and the housing market, then forecasting models that are built on prior market patterns may fail to accurately predict price changes.

Methodology

Data and Analytical Approaches

The analysis uses monthly data from January 2000 to April 2024, which provides 292 months of data. All data was retrieved from the Federal Reserve Economic Data (FRED) database, which includes house price indices from 20 different metro regions in the U.S., national unemployment rates, and national building permits authorized in the prior month. The panel structure of this data yielded a final dataset of 5,840 total observations.

The dependent variable is the month-to-month percentage change in nominal housing prices. The independent variables are the national unemployment rate change from month-to-month, the percentage change of building permits authorized month to month, a classification variable to account for the metro regions' overall growth across the 24-year sample, and period variables to account for when the observation took place. The classification variable for growth was calculated using the total appreciation of the housing index from a given metro region, split using a 200% appreciation threshold, which yielded 12 high-growth cities and 8 low-growth cities.

Analytical Approach

To complete this analysis, two different approaches were employed: frequentist and Bayesian. Panel regression was utilized alongside interaction terms, which allowed unemployment and permit effects to vary across the three defined time periods. Cluster-robust standard errors were used to account for heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation caused by relationships within the same city. The Bayesian approach utilizes the BayesFactor package to identify the ideal model configurations, completed with 10,000 iterations to generate posterior samples and identify high density intervals (HDIs) at the 95% level. Using both approaches allowed for cross-validation and drawing substantial conclusions across differing methodologies.

The model is specified as:

ΔPriceit = β₀ + β₁ΔUnemployment + β₂Recovery × ΔUnemployment + β₃COVID × ΔUnemployment
    + β₄ΔPermits + β₅Recovery × ΔPermits + β₆COVID × ΔPermits + β₇GrowthGroup + ε
Results - Frequentist

Panel Regression: A 97% Decline in Unemployment Sensitivity

The panel regression revealed statistically significant results (p<.01), with each coefficient being statistically significant individually. Pre-Crisis markets exhibited strong unemployment sensitivity, with a one percentage point increase in unemployment leading to a 2.19% monthly decline in housing prices, holding all else constant. This sensitivity weakened substantially during the Recovery period to 1.23%, holding all else constant. Then in the Covid period a nearly complete decoupling effect occurred, with unemployment effects reduced to just 0.06%, representing a 97% decline in sensitivity compared to Pre-Crisis levels. Additionally, the building permit variables were associated with a positive monthly price change, however this relationship too was reduced across the periods. The R-squared was about 15.2%, meaning that about 15.2% of the variance in monthly price change was captured by the model, which is sufficient for this analysis given the nature of monthly economic data.

Results - Bayesian

Bayesian Confirmation: The Covid HDI Contains Zero

The Bayesian findings confirm what was found in the frequentist results, with the decoupling effect for unemployment across the periods being observed using the posterior means and the HDIs. The Bayes Factor comparing the three-period interaction model with simpler configurations displayed a value of about 6.8x10^195.

The three-period model demonstrated unemployment sensitivity of -2.18% in the Pre-Crisis period, declining to -1.23% during Recovery, and further weakening to -0.06% in the Covid period. Although the Covid period does show a negative effect between unemployment and housing prices, this effect may actually be zero, as shown by the 95% HDI containing 0%. Basically, one may say that there is a 95% probability that the unemployment rate and housing prices have completely decoupled by this time period. The consistency between the analytical approaches further strengthens the results and identified patterns.

Discussion & Conclusion

A Transformation in Housing Market Dynamics

This paper revealed a transformation in how U.S. housing markets react to economic fundamentals, specifically unemployment, across varying crises, signaling a decoupling effect. The 97% reduction in unemployment sensitivity from the Pre-Crisis period to the Covid period demonstrates that forever evolving policy interventions may fundamentally alter housing price relationships. Both the frequentist and Bayesian results confirm these findings with very statistically significant results.

These findings suggest important revisions to forecasting models and policy design methodology, as housing market models may assume a straightforward effect between unemployment and prices. Future improvements and further research could include whether these decoupling effects persist past the 2020-2024 period and how current policy intervention may impact prices within housing markets for both now and the future.

Core Finding

The unemployment rate's predictive power over housing prices declined by around 97% across the three periods, from -2.19% in Pre-Crisis to -0.06% in the Covid period. This finding was supported by both the frequentist and Bayesian approaches, and these results suggest that fiscal and monetary policy intervention measures very well may have altered housing market dynamics since 2000, suggesting a decoupling effect between unemployment and housing prices.
